The thing to notice

Your machine sent one question and got one answer. Everything else was the resolver walking the tree on your behalf, one delegation at a time. Only the last server it reached was authoritative for the name. Every other one just said not me, ask them.

Notice where the web server sits. Your traffic never passes through a nameserver — they only hand out directions. That is why the line to the site cannot be drawn until the walk finishes: until then, you do not know the address to draw it to.

The thing to notice

The walk is the same. What changed is the question: it has a type, and the type decides what comes back. Every referral you watched in level 1 was made of NS records and the glue addresses that make them usable — read a message, or click the nameserver that sent it, and you are looking at the exact data the tree runs on.

Three answers behave differently. A CNAME is not an address at all, it is another name, so the resolver throws away its progress and starts again at the root. An MX names a mail server rather than a web server, which is why a second destination appeared below your machine — and its answer is a name too, so it needs a lookup of its own. Ask for NS or SOA and nothing lights up at the bottom: knowing who runs a zone does not give you anywhere to connect.

The SOA is the one you get when a name does not exist. Its last number is how long every resolver is told to remember the absence — a typo is cached exactly like a success.

The thing to notice

Ask for anu.edu.au, then ask again. The second time nothing leaves the resolver at all. Now ask for terra-web.anu.edu.au — a name it has never seen — and watch where the walk starts. The root is not asked. The TLD is not asked. The resolver already knew who serves anu.edu.au, because it cached the referral, not just the answer.

That is the whole reason the thirteen root servers can carry the internet. Then try unsw.edu.au: a different zone under the same TLD, so the root is still skipped but the TLD has to be asked. The saving is partial, and it is proportional to what was already known.

Switch to the other machine and ask the same name. It gets the cached answer too — it never spoke to a nameserver in its life. A resolver is shared infrastructure, and so is its memory. Hold onto that: in level 4 it becomes the reason one forged record is worth forging.

Nothing here propagates. Open the resolver and press wait, then watch entries drop out of its memory as their TTLs run down. A change you make to a zone is not pushed anywhere — the world simply stops remembering the old answer, at a speed you chose in advance when you set the TTL.

The thing to notice

Every message so far has been believed on the strength of one thing: a 16-bit number the resolver picked and the reply quoted back. There is no signature, no password and no return address that cannot be written by anyone. Pick on-path and resolve a name: you can read that number off the wire, so you never guess, and you win every time. The cost of this attack is not cleverness, it is standing on the link — open wifi, a router, an ISP.

Now pick off-path, where you cannot see the query and have to guess. Ask for anu.edu.au and you will almost certainly lose — and then you have lost for good, because the real answer is now cached and the next question never leaves the resolver. One shot per name, then wait out the TTL.

So do not attack the name. Press send a forged reply: it asks for a random subdomain that nobody has ever looked up and nobody has cached, so you get another attempt, and another. And do not forge the address — forge the delegation. Win once and the resolver believes you are the nameserver for the entire zone. This is Kaminsky's attack, and the reason it works is level 3's reason: a cache does not record where a record came from.

Then switch to the other machine and ask for anu.edu.au. It has never been attacked, never spoken to you, and it goes where you tell it — from a cache it never filled. The efficiency argument of level 3, running in reverse: everything that made one answer serve thousands of people makes one lie do the same.

The defences are not decoration. Port randomisation makes the reply match a port as well as an ID, squaring the search space rather than doubling it — try the attack again and watch the odds. DNSSEC is different in kind: the forgery still arrives, still quotes the right ID, and is thrown out anyway, because the records are signed and yours are not. And DNS-over-HTTPS, which is what most people mean when they say their DNS is encrypted, protects only the hop between your machine and the resolver.
