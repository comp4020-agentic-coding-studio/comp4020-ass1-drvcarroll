Who do you trust?

Every site you have ever opened began with your machine asking a stranger where is this? and believing the answer. Watch what actually happens.

Any real domain. A name that does not exist genuinely returns NXDOMAIN — try one and watch where the walk stops. Names are shown fully qualified, with the trailing root dot that DNS really uses and nobody ever types.

The thing to notice

Your machine sent one question and got one answer. Everything else was the resolver walking the tree on your behalf, one delegation at a time. Only the last server it reached was authoritative for the name. Every other one just said not me, ask them.

Notice where the web server sits. Your traffic never passes through a nameserver — they only hand out directions. That is why the line to the site cannot be drawn until the walk finishes: until then, you do not know the address to draw it to.

The thing to notice

The walk is the same. What changed is the question: it has a type, and the type decides what comes back. Every referral you watched in level 1 was made of NS records and the glue addresses that make them usable — expand a step and you are looking at the exact data the tree runs on.

Three answers behave differently. A CNAME is not an address at all, it is another name, so the resolver throws away its progress and starts again at the root. An MX names a mail server rather than a web server, which is why a second destination appeared below your machine — and its answer is a name too, so it needs a lookup of its own. Ask for NS or SOA and nothing lights up at the bottom: knowing who runs a zone does not give you anywhere to connect.

The SOA is the one you get when a name does not exist. Its last number is how long every resolver is told to remember the absence — a typo is cached exactly like a success.

The thing to notice

Ask for www.anu.edu.au, then ask again. The second time nothing leaves the resolver at all. Now ask for mail.anu.edu.au — a name it has never seen — and watch where the walk starts. The root is not asked. The TLD is not asked. The resolver already knew who serves anu.edu.au, because it cached the referral, not just the answer.

That is the whole reason the thirteen root servers can carry the internet. Then try www.unsw.edu.au: a different zone under the same TLD, so the root is still skipped but the TLD has to be asked. The saving is partial, and it is proportional to what was already known.

Switch to the other machine and ask the same name. It gets the cached answer too — it never spoke to a nameserver in its life. A resolver is shared infrastructure, and so is its memory. Hold onto that: in level 4 it becomes the reason one forged record is worth forging.

Nothing here propagates. Press wait and watch entries drop out of the table as their TTLs run down. A change you make to a zone is not pushed anywhere — the world simply stops remembering the old answer, at a speed you chose in advance when you set the TTL.
